\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to reduce the detection delay of a conformal, anytime-valid
monitor for camera/LiDAR 3D perception under real adverse weather, so that
a navigation stack could be told sooner when its perception had stopped
being trustworthy. We did not achieve that. What the attempt produced
instead are two findings about how such monitors fail silently, and a
measurement we report as unresolved rather than settled.

First, a conformal monitor can produce calibrated quantiles and plausible
alarm times while measuring nothing about the detector. When the prediction
head degenerates, the nonconformity score becomes a function of the
ground-truth labels alone and the monitor reports annotation structure as
perception degradation, with nothing in its output revealing this. The
\emph{null-detector diagnostic} --- rerun the pipeline with the prediction
head's output replaced by zeros --- exposes it for the cost of one
evaluation pass, and on our own earlier results reproduced them
bit-identically, to 17 significant figures, from a model that outputs
nothing. Content that is homogeneous within a category can make a null
detector's output look no less stable than the real pipeline's, which is
precisely why output inspection cannot substitute for the control.

Second, split conformal's exchangeability assumption fails across driving
sessions. On a drive-disjoint held-out split, per-drive miscoverage under a
pooled quantile spans $3$--$5\times$ on nominal clear-road drives ---
indicative rather than precise, given only four held-out nominal drives ---
with between-session variation comparable to the weather effect being
sought. The guarantee remains formally correct and becomes operationally
useless: the monitor detects which session the vehicle is on. For
navigation that is the worst available failure mode, because a planner
wired to a monitor that tracks route identity will act on route identity.

On the weather effect we report an underpowered null and are explicit that
this is not an absence: $+0.069$ with a 95\% interval of $[-0.073,
+0.210]$ on drive-disjoint held-out data, consistent both with a real
effect of the size we previously claimed and with none. We also show why an
earlier version appeared significant, by reporting the interval with and
without the degraded arm resampled --- a decomposition we recommend for
clustered estimates generally, because it helps distinguish an underpowered
design from a genuine absence where a single interval cannot.

Three directions follow, in the order we would pursue them. The most
promising is already identified: CADC publishes a quantitative per-drive
snowfall-severity count that varies within collection date, which would
replace the date-aliased binary contrast used here with a dose-response
regression --- more efficient statistically, and free of the confound that
limits every effect estimate in this paper. Second, the dataset releases
substantially more drives than we downloaded, and since the number of
evaluation sessions is the binding constraint on every interval reported
here, raising it addresses the limitation more directly than any change of
estimator. Third, the null-detector diagnostic should be generalized from a
single check into a family: a null per score family, and ideally a graded
sensitivity profile obtained by injecting known degradations of known
magnitude, so that a null result acquires a measured detection floor rather
than resting on an instrument of unknown sensitivity.

We would emphasise one point above the others. Every failure in this work
was silent --- a collapsed detector, an evaluation harness whose replicates
were identical, a monitor reading annotation statistics, calibration valid
in aggregate and broken per session, and a train/evaluation overlap that
made every conformal score in-sample. In each case the outputs looked
reasonable, and in each case what exposed the problem was a control:
zeroing the detector, asserting that replicates differ, measuring per
session rather than in aggregate, asserting that train and evaluation
frames are disjoint. For a monitoring system, whose whole purpose is to be
trusted when nothing else is watching, we think the controls deserve as
much space in the reporting as the headline numbers --- and that a
monitor whose sensitivity has never been demonstrated should not be counted
in a safety argument at all.

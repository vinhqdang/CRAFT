\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to reduce the detection delay of a conformal, anytime-valid
monitor for multimodal 3D perception under real adverse weather. We did not
succeed at that, and the reasons turned out to be more informative than the
target.

Three results stand. First, a conformal monitor built on a degenerate
nonconformity score produces calibrated quantiles, plausible alarm times
and clean-looking operating curves while measuring nothing about the
detector; the \emph{null-detector diagnostic} --- one extra evaluation pass
with the prediction head zeroed --- exposes this, and on our own earlier
results it reproduced every reported detection delay exactly, to the frame,
on two datasets. Second, split conformal's exchangeability assumption fails
across driving sessions, with a $7.5\times$ spread in per-drive miscoverage
under a pooled quantile and between-session variation as large as the
weather effect itself; Mondrian session-conditional calibration repairs
this on one checkpoint, though the advantage reverses on another, so we
report it as model-dependent. Third, monitorability is non-monotone in
detector quality: the measurable weather effect peaks at intermediate
quality and declines to undetectability as the detector converges, with two
structurally different nonconformity scores agreeing on the trend.

On the original objective we are unambiguous. The best configuration we
found controls false alarms where the standard approach does not --- the
standard approach alarms \emph{before} onset, accumulating on session
identity --- but detects roughly $22\%$ of onsets, which on detection rate
alone a null detector matches. Longer windows raise detection to a plateau
and then break false-alarm control. At the effect sizes available here,
reliable detection is not achievable, and no combination of calibration
scheme, betting rule, block size or window length that we tried changes
that. The monitor we can build is valid; it is not reliable.

Four directions follow. The most direct concerns score design: if a
converged detector's residual degradation is real but concentrated in rare,
severe failures, a frame-averaged miscoverage rate dilutes it away, and a
score targeting the tail rather than the mean might recover what we could
not. Our phantom-score result argues weakly against the simplest version of
this, but does not settle it. Second, the non-monotonicity should be tested
on another architecture and another degradation mode before it is treated
as general; we establish it on one detector, one dataset and one axis of
quality variation. Third, the Mondrian reversal wants an explanation, since
a calibration scheme whose benefit depends on the quality of the model
being calibrated is a caveat worth understanding rather than merely
recording. Fourth, the spatial construction --- per-cell e-processes under
multiplicity control --- remains untested against ground-truth localized
degradation, because neither dataset annotates where degradation occurs.

We would emphasise one practical point over the rest. Throughout this work
the failures were silent: a collapsed detector, a degenerate evaluation
harness whose replicates were all identical, a monitor detecting annotation
statistics, a calibration scheme failing per session while valid on
average. In every case the outputs looked reasonable, and in every case
what exposed the problem was a control --- zeroing the detector, asserting
replicates differ, measuring per session rather than in aggregate,
inserting a temporal gap. For monitoring systems specifically, whose entire
purpose is to be trusted when nothing else is watching, we think the
controls deserve as much attention in reporting as the headline numbers.

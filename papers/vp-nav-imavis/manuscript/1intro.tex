\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A deployed autonomous-vehicle perception stack is validated under some set
of sensing conditions and then asked to operate outside them. The failure
that matters operationally is not that the detector is sometimes wrong ---
it always is --- but that it becomes wrong in a way nothing downstream is
told about. Conformal prediction offers a distribution-free way to attach
calibrated uncertainty to a black-box detector's outputs, and anytime-valid
sequential testing turns that into a live monitor: calibrate a
nonconformity score on nominal data, track its miscoverage $m(t)$ on the
incoming stream, and raise an alarm when a betting process accumulates
enough evidence against the nominal hypothesis. The construction is
attractive because its false-alarm guarantee holds uniformly over all
stopping times, so the question ``has anything changed yet?'' can be asked
continuously without a multiple-testing penalty.

We set out to apply that recipe to multimodal 3D detection under real
adverse weather, and specifically to improve its detection delay. We did
not achieve that, and this paper is largely about why the attempt was more
instructive than the goal. Three findings emerged that we think are more
useful than a delay improvement would have been, and we state the negative
result on the original objective plainly rather than burying it.

\paragraph{A monitor can be entirely fictitious and look fine.}
The nonconformity score in this setting is a residual between predicted and
ground-truth boxes, evaluated at cells a ground-truth object occupies. If
the detector's regression head collapses --- as ours did, under a
placeholder training loss whose optimum is a constant --- that residual
degenerates to a function of the ground-truth labels alone. The monitor
then measures the distribution of scene content, not detector error, and
because scene content genuinely differs between a dataset's clear and
adverse splits, it produces detection delays that look completely
reasonable. We introduce a \emph{null-detector diagnostic} that catches
this: rerun the entire pipeline with the regression head's output replaced
by zeros. It requires no additional data, training or annotation. Applied
to our own earlier results, it reproduced every reported detection delay
\emph{exactly}, to the frame, on both datasets --- a monitor with no
detector performing identically to one with a detector
(Section~\ref{sec:null-diagnostic}). We think this control belongs in any
conformal monitoring study, and we foreground it as a contribution in its
own right rather than as an internal check.

\paragraph{Exchangeability fails across driving sessions.}
Split conformal calibration assumes calibration and test data are
exchangeable. Real driving data is collected in sessions, and sessions
differ in ways that survive calibration: on the Canadian Adverse Driving
Conditions dataset (CADC)~\cite{Pitropov2021CADC} we measure per-drive mean
miscoverage ranging from $0.083$ to $0.623$ against a target of $0.20$ ---
a $7.5\times$ spread across drives that are all nominal, all clear-road,
several from a single collection date. The between-session standard
deviation is as large as the weather effect the monitor exists to detect,
so under pooled calibration the monitored quantity is dominated by which
session the vehicle is on. Mondrian conformal
prediction~\cite{VovkMondrian2003}, conditioning calibration on the session,
repairs this on one checkpoint --- but, as we report in
Section~\ref{sec:mondrian-reversal}, that advantage reverses on another, so
we present it as model-dependent rather than as a general property.

\paragraph{Monitorability is non-monotone in detector quality.}
This is the paper's central claim and the most counterintuitive. Measuring
the weather effect across a series of checkpoints spanning a real quality
range, the effect is largest at intermediate detector quality and declines
monotonically as the detector improves, becoming statistically undetectable
beyond a heatmap object-versus-empty separation of roughly $0.41$
(Section~\ref{sec:quality-curve}). A collapsed detector yields only content
artifacts; a mid-quality detector yields real, attributable weather signal;
a converged detector yields none, because it degrades gracefully enough
under snow that little error differential remains. Two structurally
different nonconformity scores --- one restricted to ground-truth object
cells and therefore blind to spurious detections, one built entirely from
activation in empty space --- agree on where signal exists, which is
stronger evidence than either could provide alone.

The deployment implication is uncomfortable and, we think, worth stating
directly: \emph{improving your detector can silently disable your monitor}.
The monitor does not fail loudly when this happens. It continues to emit
calibrated-looking quantiles and plausible alarm times, and without a
control of the kind described above there is no signal that anything is
wrong.

\paragraph{What we do not claim.}
We are explicit about statistical power throughout rather than confining it
to a limitations section. The quality curve has six points, of which only
two reach significance with $18$ sessions; the claim rests on the monotone
effect-size trend and the loss of detectability, not on a run of
significant measurements. One of the six --- the collapsed checkpoint ---
is not a commensurable measurement at all, since its score is a
demonstrated content artifact, and we mark it as such rather than fitting a
trend through it. On the original objective, detection at these effect
sizes is not achievable: the best configuration we found controls false
alarms where the baseline does not, but detects roughly $22\%$ of onsets,
no better than a null detector (Section~\ref{sec:operating-curve}). We
report that with intervals attached.

Snowy Scenes~\cite{SnowyScenes2026} appears throughout as a negative
control. Its real detector shows no weather effect distinguishable from
zero, while its null detector shows a large one --- the mirror image of
CADC --- and that contrast is what makes CADC's mid-quality result
credible rather than a diagnostic artifact of our own pipeline.

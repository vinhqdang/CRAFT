\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Every number in this section is produced by a script committed alongside
the manuscript and written to a JSON artifact named in the corresponding
caption, so each table can be regenerated and checked against its source.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

We evaluate on two real adverse-weather driving datasets. CADC
\cite{Pitropov2021CADC} provides $18$ nominal (\texttt{bare}-road) driving
sessions and $14$ snow-covered sessions, with the road-condition label
taken from the dataset's own per-drive metadata rather than inferred.
Snowy Scenes~\cite{SnowyScenes2026} provides real snowy driving across
three weather categories; since it contains no clear-weather split, its
nominal/degraded contrast is constructed across categories using a measured
per-point snow-class fraction, and it serves here as a negative control
rather than as a second positive result.

The monitored detector is a camera/LiDAR fusion architecture producing a
per-cell class heatmap and box regression on a $128 \times 128$
bird's-eye-view grid. The nonconformity score is the $\ell_1$
box-regression residual at cells a ground-truth object occupies; a second
score, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:phantom-score}, is built from
predicted activation at cells that contain no ground-truth object. All
monitoring uses $\alpha = 0.2$.

\paragraph{Evaluation harness.} Onset streams splice a nominal prefix with
a degraded suffix at a known onset index. Frames within a stream are
sampled without replacement from the relevant pool under a per-replicate
seed, and every arm of every comparison reuses one shared seed list, so
methods are compared on identical frame draws replicate by replicate. This
matters: an earlier version of this harness constructed streams by modular
arithmetic with no sampling, so all replicates of a condition were the
identical stream and every reported statistic was a single sample measured
$n$ times. The signature was visible in the outputs --- false-alarm rates
landing on exactly $0.00$ or $1.00$ and censoring on exactly $0/5$ or $5/5$
--- and a regression test now asserts that replicates differ.

\paragraph{Reporting conventions.} Detection-delay comparisons are gated on
false-alarm control: a monitor whose empirical false-alarm rate exceeds
$\delta$ has no valid delay, because it can appear fast by alarming
indiscriminately, including before the onset exists. Negative delays in the
tables below denote exactly that pathology. Confidence intervals are
percentile bootstrap; where sessions are the unit of analysis they are
resampled over sessions rather than frames, since frames within a drive are
strongly correlated and frame-level resampling understates intervals.

\subsection{The Null-Detector Diagnostic}
\label{sec:null-diagnostic}

The diagnostic reruns the complete pipeline --- calibration included ---
with the detector's box-regression output replaced by zeros, so the
nonconformity score reduces to $\lVert B_{\mathrm{target}} \rVert_1$ and
depends only on ground-truth scene content. If a monitor's behaviour is
driven by detector error, zeroing the detector must change it.

Table~\ref{tab:null-diagnostic} applies this to the checkpoints that
produced our earlier results. On both datasets the null detector reproduces
the real detector's reported detection delays exactly, at every
false-alarm budget. On CADC the null monitor is in fact \emph{better
behaved} than the real one, holding a $0.00$ false-alarm rate where the
real monitor's exceeds every budget.

The null delays of $6/8/9$ on CADC and $3/10$ on Snowy Scenes are, to the
frame, the numbers we had previously reported as real detection results.
The explanation is direct: those checkpoints had collapsed under a
placeholder detection loss, so they \emph{were} null detectors. A separate
measurement makes this exact rather than inferred --- on the collapsed
checkpoint the nominal-to-degraded jump in $m$ is
$-0.21573659$, matching the zeros branch to eight decimal places, with an
artifact-subtracted residual of exactly $0.00000$
(\texttt{quality\_effect\_curve\_cadc.json}).

We draw a methodological conclusion beyond our own case. A conformal
monitor built on a degenerate score does not fail conspicuously; it reports
calibrated quantiles and plausible alarm times throughout. The null-detector
control costs one extra evaluation pass and distinguishes the two cases
unambiguously, and we suggest it be reported as standard.

\subsection{Session-Conditional Calibration, and Where It Reverses}
\label{sec:mondrian-reversal}

Mondrian calibration conditions the quantile on the session. This requires
a within-session split --- each drive calibrates on its own leading frames
and is monitored on the remainder --- because under the whole-drive split a
monitored drive has no calibration frames and no per-session quantile
exists for it. That arrangement is deployment-faithful (a vehicle
calibrates on the early known-nominal portion of the drive it is on) but it
places calibration and monitored frames adjacent in time, and adjacent
driving frames are correlated. We therefore insert a temporal gap and sweep
it; an effect inflated by adjacency would shrink as the gap grows.

On the 5-epoch checkpoint Mondrian improves effect-to-noise by $1.54\times$
and more than halves the worst-case per-session deviation, and the effect
is stable across gaps ($+0.074$ at gap $0$, $+0.087$ at gap $20$), so
adjacency is not inflating it. That worst-case improvement is the mechanism
by which false-alarm control returns, since a $0.41$ deviation under pooled
calibration is what drives clear-stream alarms.

The converged checkpoint reverses the worst-case ordering: Mondrian is
roughly $1.7\times$ \emph{worse} than marginal. We do not have an
explanation for this and do not offer one. It means the benefit of
session-conditional calibration here is a property of the
model--data combination rather than an unconditional improvement, and we
state it that way: any claim of the form ``Mondrian improves worst-case
per-session coverage'' is contradicted by our own second measurement.

\subsection{Block Aggregation and the Temporal-Correlation Penalty}
\label{sec:blocking}

Averaging $m$ over blocks of $k$ frames before the betting step cannot
change the session-level statistic --- averaging within a session leaves
the session mean unchanged, so the session-level effect and between-session
standard deviation are exactly invariant to $k$, which we verify to six
decimal places as a correctness check on the implementation. What blocking
reduces is the within-session per-block variance, the noise a sequential
test consumes when judging whether the current stretch of frames looks
degraded.

Blocking delivers roughly half the gain independent sampling would predict
--- $1.42\times$ at $k=8$ against $2.83\times$ --- which quantifies the
temporal-correlation penalty for $10$\,Hz driving frames. At $k=8$ the
effect itself begins to erode ($+0.0659 \to +0.0614$) as blocks span
genuinely different scene content, so the gain turns over; we use $k=4$.
The null detector's discriminability stays flat and low across all $k$
($0.128$, $0.133$, $0.128$, $0.100$), confirming that blocking does not
manufacture discriminability where there is no signal.

\subsection{A Phantom-Detection Score}
\label{sec:phantom-score}

The localization score is restricted to ground-truth object cells and is
therefore structurally blind to spurious detections in empty space --- which
is snow's documented physical effect on LiDAR. We add a second score family:
predicted peak activation at cells containing no ground-truth object,
calibrated by the same split-conformal procedure at level $\alpha$, with the
frame rate a convex combination
$m = (1-w)\,m_{\mathrm{loc}} + w\,m_{\mathrm{phantom}}$. Each component has
expectation at most $\alpha$ under the nominal distribution, so the
combination does too, and $w=0$ recovers the original score exactly.

The effect grows monotonically with phantom weight, $54\%$ larger at $w=1$
than at $w=0$, and every cell survives the gap control. Mixing beats either
endpoint on effect-to-noise ($0.862$ at $w=0.5$), so the two score families
are partly complementary rather than redundant: phantom activations carry
weather information the localization score cannot see. On the converged
checkpoint, by contrast, no weight at any gap produces an effect whose
interval excludes zero (\texttt{phantom\_effect\_converged.json}).

We flag a limitation of this arm specifically. The null-detector control
used elsewhere zeroes the box head, which leaves the heatmap untouched and
is therefore not a null for a score that reads the heatmap. Zeroing all
heads makes every phantom score identical and the component degenerate ---
a floor by construction rather than an informative comparison. For $w > 0$
the gap sweep and the confidence intervals carry the evidential weight, and
we do not present the all-heads-null row as a control.

\paragraph{Power, stated before the interpretation.} Only two of the six
rows reach significance, and the session-level bootstrap has $18$
independent units, so intervals are wide. The claim below rests on the
monotone decline in effect size across the five trained checkpoints and on
the loss of detectability beyond separation $\approx 0.41$, \emph{not} on a
sequence of significant measurements. A reader who requires significance at
every point should regard this as suggestive.

With that stated: the phantom effect declines monotonically across all five
trained checkpoints, $+0.1077 \to +0.0074$, as separation rises from $0.25$
to $0.48$. The localization effect broadly tracks it, from $+0.0660$ to
$-0.0126$, though not strictly monotonically --- epoch 16 ($+0.0353$) sits
above epoch 13 ($+0.0116$), and neither is significant. The two scores
agree on significance at five of six points; they disagree at epoch 9,
where localization is significant and phantom is not. We report the
disagreement rather than smoothing it: at that point the two score families
give different answers about whether signal remains.

The collapsed checkpoint's apparent phantom effect of $+0.1562$ is the
largest in the table and is not detector signal at all. Including it in a
fitted trend would be a category error, which is why it is separated above.

Two things hold. The baseline is invalid at $\delta \le 0.10$ and its
delays are \emph{negative} --- it alarms before the onset exists, and does
so progressively earlier as the window lengthens ($-3.4$ at $20$ frames,
$-9.4$ at $40$, $-17.3$ at $80$), which is the signature of accumulating on
session identity. Our arm controls false alarms at every budget where the
baseline does not, and the null detector is invalid at $\delta \le 0.10$,
so where our arm is valid and detecting, the detection is attributable.

One thing does not hold, and it is the original objective. Detection rate
is $31\%$, $22\%$ and $17\%$ --- and on detection rate alone the null
detector matches it ($8/36$ versus $8/36$ at $\delta = 0.10$). Longer
windows do not fix this: across $20/40/60/80$ frames detection rises from
$8\%$ to $22\%$ and then plateaus, while false-alarm control breaks at $80$
frames as the extra frames give the clear streams more opportunity to cross
the threshold. We therefore make no performance claim. The monitor we can
build is \emph{valid} where the standard one is not; it is not
\emph{reliable}.

\subsection{Snowy Scenes as Negative Control}
\label{sec:snowy-control}

On Snowy Scenes the real detector's weather effect is $-0.0085$ with a 95\%
CI of $[-0.0719, +0.0553]$ --- centred on zero --- while the null detector's
is $-0.1315$, $[-0.1917, -0.0726]$, clearly non-zero
(\texttt{effect\_exists\_snowy.json}). The only signal present is a content
artifact pointing the wrong way, which is why the null monitor ``detected''
at $3$ and $10$ frames there while the real one was censored throughout.

This is the mirror image of CADC, where the real effect is present and the
null's is not, and the contrast is what makes the CADC result credible: the
same diagnostic applied to two datasets returns opposite verdicts, so it is
discriminating between them rather than reporting a property of our
pipeline.

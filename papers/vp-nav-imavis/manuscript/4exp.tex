\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Every number below is produced by a script committed alongside the
manuscript and written to a JSON artifact named in the corresponding
caption.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

CADC~\cite{Pitropov2021CADC} provides 32 downloaded driving sessions across
three collection dates: 18 nominal (\texttt{bare}-road) and 14
snow-covered. Snowy Scenes~\cite{SnowyScenes2026} provides real snowy
driving; it contains no clear-weather split, so its contrast is constructed
across weather categories and it serves here as a negative control rather
than a second positive result.

\paragraph{Train/evaluation separation.} The detector and the monitor are
built on disjoint \emph{drives}. Frame-level holdout would not suffice:
consecutive CADC frames are ${\sim}10$\,Hz samples of one scene, so a
frame-level split leaves near-duplicates of training frames in evaluation.
Drives are interleaved within each weather category, giving 16 training and
16 evaluation drives (1534 / 1408 frames) with both categories and all
three dates represented on each side. Calibration and monitoring both draw
only from evaluation drives, so the conformal scores are out-of-sample.

This matters because an earlier version of this work did not do it. The
detector and the monitor were constructed over one unsplit dataset object,
so every calibration, nominal and degraded frame had been a training frame,
and the nonconformity scores were in-sample residuals against which a
calibrated quantile bounds nothing. A regression test now asserts that the
train and evaluation frame-id sets are disjoint.

\paragraph{Nesting with session-conditional calibration.} Mondrian
calibration needs calibration frames from inside each monitored session, so
an evaluation drive is split again internally into a leading calibration
prefix, a temporal gap, and a monitored remainder. That inner split lives
entirely within evaluation drives and never reaches training data.

\paragraph{Inference.} Sessions, not frames, are the unit of analysis:
frames within a drive are strongly correlated and frame-level resampling
understates intervals badly. Where an estimate contrasts nominal against
degraded data, \emph{both} arms are resampled --- nominal sessions and
degraded drives --- because the degraded side carries real between-drive
variation (measured sd $0.057$) that a fixed pool would silently treat as
zero. All intervals are percentile bootstrap.

\subsection{The Null-Detector Diagnostic}
\label{sec:null-diagnostic}

The diagnostic reruns the entire pipeline, calibration included, with the
detector's box-regression output replaced by zeros. The nonconformity score
then reduces to $\lVert B_{\mathrm{target}} \rVert_1$ and depends only on
ground-truth scene content. If a monitor's behaviour reflects detector
error, zeroing the detector must change it.

Table~\ref{tab:null-diagnostic} gives the result on the checkpoints that
produced our earlier numbers. The agreement is exact, not approximate: the
detector contributes nothing measurable to the monitor's evidence, because
those checkpoints had collapsed under a placeholder detection loss and
\emph{were} null detectors. This is the cleanest available statement of the
finding, and it is immune to any question about evaluation-harness
configuration, since it compares two branches of a single deterministic
computation.

We draw a conclusion beyond our own case. A conformal monitor built on a
degenerate score does not fail conspicuously --- it reports calibrated
quantiles and plausible alarm times throughout. One extra evaluation pass
distinguishes the two situations unambiguously. We suggest it be reported
as standard, with one scope condition stated honestly: this is a null for
scores built from regression residuals. A score reading a different output
head needs its own null, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} explains why
constructing one is not always straightforward.

Marginal validity holds on average while failing per session. Four of nine

its alarms carry route information rather than perception information.

\subsection{Session-Conditional Calibration, and Where It Reverses}
\label{sec:mondrian}

Mondrian conformal prediction~\cite{VovkMondrian2003} conditions the
quantile on a taxonomy; here the taxonomy is the driving session.

\midrule
\multicolumn{5}{@{}l}{\emph{converged checkpoint}} \\
0 & $-0.033$ & $-0.025$ & $\mathbf{0.187}$ & $0.344$ \\
10 & $-0.104$ & $-0.079$ & $\mathbf{0.221}$ & $0.363$ \\
20 & $0.096$ & $0.003$ & $\mathbf{0.210}$ & $0.353$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

On the 5-epoch checkpoint session-conditional calibration more than halves
the worst-case per-session deviation. On the converged checkpoint the
ordering reverses and Mondrian is roughly $1.7\times$ worse. We do not have
an explanation and do not offer one. Two consequences follow, and we state
both rather than the convenient one. First, the benefit is a property of
the model--data combination, not an unconditional improvement: any claim
that Mondrian improves worst-case per-session coverage is contradicted by
our own second measurement. Second, neither the benefit nor the reversal
carries an interval --- $\max_s |m_s - \alpha|$ is a maximum over nine
sessions, among the least stable estimators available --- so the reversal
may itself be noise. A practitioner should measure this on their own model
rather than assume either direction.

\subsection{The Weather Effect: an Underpowered Null}
\label{sec:underpowered}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Nominal-versus-degraded effect on drive-disjoint held-out drives
(9 nominal sessions, 7 degraded drives), Mondrian calibration. The
two-level interval resamples nominal sessions and degraded drives; the
nominal-only interval resamples sessions alone, from the same draws, and is
shown so the effect of the correction is visible. Artifact:
\texttt{split\_effect\_split\_ep5.json}.}
\label{tab:underpowered}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
gap & effect & two-level 95\% CI & nominal-only 95\% CI & inflation & null detector \\
\midrule
0 & $+0.064$ & $[-0.061, +0.193]$ & $[-0.031, +0.143]$ & $1.46\times$ & n.s. \\
10 & $+0.076$ & $[-0.059, +0.211]$ & $[-0.029, +0.161]$ & $1.43\times$ & n.s. \\
20 & $+0.069$ & $[-0.073, +0.210]$ & $[-0.039, +0.163]$ & $1.40\times$ & n.s. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The effect is not distinguishable from zero at any gap, under either
calibration scheme, and the null detector is likewise null throughout.

\paragraph{This is an underpowered null, not a demonstrated absence.} The
interval $[-0.061, +0.193]$ is consistent with a real effect of the size we
previously claimed and equally consistent with none. With nine evaluation
sessions and a between-session sd near $0.06$, the minimum detectable
effect at 80\% power is roughly $0.13$ --- about twice the point estimate.
The design cannot resolve an effect of this magnitude, and no conclusion
about the presence or absence of weather-induced degradation should be
drawn from it.

\paragraph{Why an earlier version appeared significant.} Before correction
this measurement read $+0.074$ with an interval of $[+0.020, +0.117]$. The
point estimate barely moved --- $+0.074 \rightarrow +0.064$, a 14\%
reduction --- so removing the train/evaluation overlap did \emph{not}
destroy the effect. Significance was lost for two separate statistical
reasons, and reporting both intervals separates them. Restricting to
held-out drives cut the session count from 18 to 9, which alone widened the
interval enough to cross zero (the nominal-only column). Resampling the
degraded arm widened it a further $1.4$--$1.5\times$. Neither correction
alone would have exposed the problem, and the earlier significance was an
artifact of the two compounding.

We suggest reporting both intervals as general practice for clustered
effect estimates. A single interval tells a reader whether a result is
significant; the pair tells them \emph{why} it is not, which is the
difference between concluding ``underpowered'' and concluding ``absent.''

\subsection{Snowy Scenes as Negative Control}
\label{sec:snowy-control}

On Snowy Scenes the real detector's effect is $-0.0085$ with a 95\%
interval of $[-0.0719, +0.0553]$, centred on zero, while the null
detector's is $-0.1315$, $[-0.1917, -0.0726]$
(\texttt{effect\_exists\_snowy.json}). The only signal present is a content
artifact pointing the wrong way, which is why the null monitor ``detected''
there while the real one did not.

This is the mirror image of CADC, where the real effect is the larger and
the null's interval covers zero. The same diagnostic returns opposite
verdicts on two datasets, which is the evidence that it discriminates
between them rather than reporting a property of our pipeline. We note that
these two intervals were computed with frame-level rather than
session-level resampling and are therefore narrower than our convention
elsewhere would give; the qualitative contrast is robust to this, but the
Snowy interval widths should not be read as comparable to
Table~\ref{tab:underpowered}'s.

\subsection{What We Do Not Report}
\label{sec:not-reported}

Three lines of work were carried far enough to have numbers and are
excluded, because the numbers do not support conclusions.

A curve relating measured weather effect to detector quality across
checkpoints is omitted: its quality axis (heatmap object-versus-empty
separation) shares a term with one of the two scores plotted against it ---
the phantom score \emph{is} the empty-cell activation that the axis
subtracts --- so a decline along that axis is partly circular. Its inputs
also predate the corrections in Section~\ref{sec:exp-setup}. We record it
as an open question, not a finding.

A covariate-informed betting rule conditioned on the detector's own
cross-modal consistency score, and a spatially resolved variant using
per-cell e-processes under multiplicity control, are described in
Section~\ref{sec:method} because they motivate the design, but neither is
evaluated here and neither carries a result in this paper.

Operating-curve and detection-delay results are omitted because, under the
corrected design, no configuration has a demonstrable false-alarm
advantage: with 36 replicates, an observed rate of $0/36$ has an exact
upper confidence bound of $0.080$, which does not establish control at
$\delta = 0.05$.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Every number below is produced by a script committed alongside the
manuscript and written to a JSON artifact named in the corresponding
caption.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

CADC~\cite{Pitropov2021CADC} provides 32 downloaded driving sessions across
three collection dates: 18 nominal (\texttt{bare}-road) and 14
snow-covered. Snowy Scenes~\cite{SnowyScenes2026} provides real snowy
driving; it contains no clear-weather split, so its contrast is constructed
across weather categories and it serves here as a negative control rather
than a second positive result.

\paragraph{Train/evaluation separation.} The detector and the monitor are
built on disjoint \emph{drives}. Frame-level holdout would not suffice:
consecutive CADC frames are ${\sim}10$\,Hz samples of one scene, so a
frame-level split leaves near-duplicates of training frames in evaluation.
Drives are interleaved within each weather category, giving 16 training and
16 evaluation drives (1534 / 1408 frames) with both categories and all
three dates represented on each side. Calibration and monitoring both draw
only from evaluation drives, so the conformal scores are out-of-sample.

This matters because an earlier version of this work did not do it. The
detector and the monitor were constructed over one unsplit dataset object,
so every calibration, nominal and degraded frame had been a training frame,
and the nonconformity scores were in-sample residuals against which a
calibrated quantile bounds nothing. A regression test now asserts that the
train and evaluation frame-id sets are disjoint.

\paragraph{Nesting with session-conditional calibration.} Mondrian
calibration needs calibration frames from inside each monitored session, so
an evaluation drive is split again internally into a leading calibration
prefix, a temporal gap, and a monitored remainder. That inner split lives
entirely within evaluation drives and never reaches training data.

\paragraph{Inference.} Sessions, not frames, are the unit of analysis:
frames within a drive are strongly correlated and frame-level resampling
understates intervals badly. Where an estimate contrasts nominal against
degraded data, \emph{both} arms are resampled --- nominal sessions and
degraded drives --- because the degraded side carries real between-drive
variation (measured sd $0.128$, Mondrian calibration) that a fixed pool
would silently treat as zero. All intervals are percentile bootstrap.

\subsection{The Null-Detector Diagnostic}
\label{sec:null-diagnostic}

The diagnostic reruns the entire pipeline, calibration included, with the
detector's box-regression output replaced by zeros. The nonconformity score
then reduces to $\lVert B_{\mathrm{target}} \rVert_1$ and depends only on
ground-truth scene content. If a monitor's behaviour reflects detector
error, zeroing the detector must change it.

Table~\ref{tab:null-diagnostic} gives the result on the checkpoints that
produced our earlier numbers. The agreement is exact, not approximate: the
detector contributes nothing measurable to the monitor's evidence, because
those checkpoints had collapsed under a placeholder detection loss and
\emph{were} null detectors. This is the cleanest available statement of the
finding, and it is immune to any question about evaluation-harness
configuration, since it compares two branches of a single deterministic
computation.

We draw a conclusion beyond our own case. A conformal monitor built on a
degenerate score does not fail conspicuously --- it reports calibrated
quantiles and plausible alarm times throughout. One extra evaluation pass
distinguishes the two situations unambiguously. We suggest it be reported
as standard, with one scope condition stated honestly: this is a null for
scores built from regression residuals. A score reading a different output
head needs its own null, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} explains why
constructing one is not always straightforward.

detecting session identity to a degree comparable with the effect it exists
to find, and its alarms carry route information rather than perception
information --- the qualitative conclusion is unchanged even though the
quantitative spread is smaller than we first reported.

\subsection{Session-Conditional Calibration: An Inconsistent Fix}
\label{sec:mondrian}

Mondrian conformal prediction~\cite{VovkMondrian2003} conditions the
quantile on a taxonomy; here the taxonomy is the driving session. It is the
natural response to Section~\ref{sec:exchangeability}'s finding, so we
evaluate it on the same drive-disjoint checkpoint, matched session for
session, frame for frame and gap for gap against marginal calibration.

\bottomrule

\end{table}

The result does not support a clean recommendation either way, and we
report it as such rather than picking the reading that flatters the method.
On worst-case per-session deviation from $\alpha$ --- the statistic Mondrian
calibration is designed to control --- it wins narrowly at gap $0$ and loses
at gaps $10$ and $20$, the opposite of what conditioning on session identity
should buy as the gap widens and the twenty calibration frames per session
have less contamination from adjacency to explain a benefit. On the ratio
that matters for detecting the weather effect itself, effect over nominal
standard deviation, Mondrian is \emph{worse} than marginal at every gap,
because splitting twenty calibration frames per session into its own
quantile estimate adds quantile-estimation noise that a single pooled
quantile does not pay. Session-conditional calibration is not a free lunch:
with this few calibration frames per session, it can trade one source of
miscalibration (between-session heterogeneity) for another
(per-session quantile-estimation noise), and which one dominates is not
obvious in advance. Neither statistic carries an interval --- $\max_s
|m_s - \alpha|$ and the effect-to-noise ratio are both computed from nine
sessions, among the least stable estimators available at this sample size
--- so a practitioner should measure this on their own model and calibration
budget rather than assume either direction from a single run.

The effect is not distinguishable from zero at any gap, under either
calibration scheme, and the null detector is likewise null throughout.

\begin{sloppypar}
\paragraph{This is an underpowered null, not a demonstrated absence.} The
interval $[-0.073, +0.210]$ is consistent with a real effect of the size we
previously claimed and equally consistent with none. We compute the
minimum detectable effect directly from this interval's bootstrap standard
error (half-width divided by $z_{0.975}$, then scaled by
$z_{0.975} + z_{0.80} \approx 2.80$ for 80\% power) rather than asserting
it: at gap $0$, MDE$_{80} \approx 0.202$ --- almost $3\times$ the point
estimate of $+0.069$ (\texttt{mde\_cadc.json}). The design cannot resolve an
effect of this magnitude, and no conclusion about the presence or absence
of weather-induced degradation should be drawn from it.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{sloppypar}
\paragraph{Why an earlier version appeared significant.} Two independent
defects were present before correction, and we can only cleanly separate
one of them. The train/evaluation overlap put the entire pipeline in-sample
over 18 nominal-regime sessions, giving $+0.074$, $[+0.020, +0.117]$
(\texttt{mondrian\_gap\_sweep\_cadc\_presplit\_insample.json}, preserved
separately from the corrected artifact of the same name). A
separate bookkeeping bug in the two-level bootstrap let each session's
first twenty frames --- its own calibration set --- re-enter the monitored
window at gap $0$, biasing that specific cell; holding the drive-disjoint
split fixed and only fixing this bug moves the gap-$0$ estimate from
$+0.064$ (\texttt{split\_effect\_split\_ep5.json}, preserved for
comparison) to $+0.069$ (\texttt{split\_effect\_split\_ep5\_fixed.json}), a
small change relative to the interval width. What we
cannot do cleanly is isolate the split correction from the bug fix in
combination, because the bug lived inside the two-level bootstrap that only
ever ran on the held-out split; the 18-session, in-sample number comes from
a different script entirely. What is well supported is this: the point
estimate moves only modestly across every correction we can check
individually ($+0.074 \rightarrow +0.064 \rightarrow +0.069$), while
significance is lost once the drive-disjoint split cuts the session count
from 18 to 9 --- even the nominal-only column, which does not resample the
degraded arm, fails to reach significance at any gap on the corrected data
(Table~\ref{tab:underpowered}, fourth column). The earlier significance
was not driven by a larger true effect; it was driven by having more
sessions than the design was entitled to.
\end{sloppypar}

We suggest reporting both intervals as general practice for clustered
effect estimates. A single interval tells a reader whether a result is
significant; the pair tells them something about \emph{why}, which is part
of the difference between concluding ``underpowered'' and concluding
``absent.''

\subsection{Snowy Scenes as Negative Control}
\label{sec:snowy-control}

On Snowy Scenes the real detector's effect is $-0.0085$ with a 95\%
interval of $[-0.0719, +0.0553]$, centred on zero, while the null
detector's is $-0.1315$, $[-0.1917, -0.0726]$
(\texttt{effect\_exists\_snowy.json}). The only signal present is a content
artifact pointing the wrong way, which is why the null monitor ``detected''
there while the real one did not.

On the corrected CADC split (Table~\ref{tab:underpowered}) neither arm is
significant: the real detector's interval covers zero and so does the null
detector's. This is not the mirror image we originally reported --- an
earlier draft, using the in-sample CADC artifact, read the real effect as
significant and the null's as covering zero, the reverse of Snowy. That
contrast does not survive the correction, and we withdraw it. What the two
datasets now show is asymmetric in a more modest but still useful way:
Snowy Scenes demonstrates that the null-detector diagnostic \emph{does}
fire on a genuine content artifact when one is present, while CADC's
corrected result shows it does not fire spuriously when the honest
answer is ``we cannot tell.'' Neither dataset gives us a case where the
real detector is significant and the null is not --- the comparison we
would need to claim the diagnostic cleanly isolates detector-driven signal
from content-driven signal in a positive instance --- and we say so rather
than reach for the reading our earlier, uncorrected numbers had offered. We
note that the Snowy intervals were computed with frame-level rather than
session-level resampling and are therefore narrower than our convention
elsewhere would give; this does not affect the qualitative reading above.

\subsection{What We Do Not Report}
\label{sec:not-reported}

Three lines of work were carried far enough to have numbers and are
excluded, because the numbers do not support conclusions.

A curve relating measured weather effect to detector quality across
checkpoints is omitted: its quality axis (heatmap object-versus-empty
separation) shares a term with one of the two scores plotted against it ---
the phantom score \emph{is} the empty-cell activation that the axis
subtracts --- so a decline along that axis is partly circular. Its inputs
also predate the corrections in Section~\ref{sec:exp-setup}. We record it
as an open question, not a finding.

A covariate-informed betting rule conditioned on the detector's own
cross-modal consistency score, and a spatially resolved variant using
per-cell e-processes under multiplicity control, are described in
Section~\ref{sec:method} because they motivate the design, but neither is
evaluated here and neither carries a result in this paper.

Operating-curve and detection-delay results are omitted because, under the
corrected design, no configuration has a demonstrable false-alarm
advantage: with 36 replicates, an observed rate of $0/36$ has an exact
upper confidence bound of $0.080$, which does not establish control at
$\delta = 0.05$.

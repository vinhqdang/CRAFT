\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why a Fictitious Monitor Looks Healthy}
\label{sec:discuss-null}

The mechanism is worth stating precisely, because it generalizes past our
particular bug. The nonconformity score is a residual evaluated where a
ground-truth object sits. When the prediction term vanishes it becomes
$\lVert B_{\mathrm{target}} \rVert_1$: a function of the labels alone.
Nothing downstream notices. Split conformal calibration still returns a
well-defined quantile, because it is the quantile of \emph{some}
exchangeable score distribution. The betting process still accumulates
wealth, because the miscoverage rate still moves between the nominal and
degraded halves of a stream. The alarm still fires at a plausible time,
because ground-truth box statistics genuinely differ between a dataset's
splits. Every component behaves exactly as specified, and the monitor
reports annotation structure as perception degradation.

We were careful about which dataset property drives this, having initially
got it wrong. We had asserted object count roughly doubling between CADC's
nominal and degraded splits, with no measurement behind the number.
Measuring it directly, on the ground-truth heatmap and on the same
nominal/degraded frames used throughout (\texttt{object\_counts\_cadc.json}),
degraded frames contain \emph{fewer} matched objects than nominal ones ---
$12.65$ versus $13.72$ per frame, a ratio of $0.92$, not the near-doubling we
first claimed. Since the score reduces to $\lVert B_{\mathrm{target}}
\rVert_1$, object count is at most a minor contributor and the driver is
more likely the distribution of ground-truth box dimensions and positions
across the two collection dates, which we have not measured directly and
report as a plausible explanation rather than a demonstrated one. It
interacts directly with the date confound discussed below.

We do not have a clean instance of the inversion we originally reported ---
a real detector that alarms while a content-only null detector stays
quiet, or vice versa --- once the false-alarm comparison is restricted to
the corrected, drive-disjoint design: Section~\ref{sec:not-reported}
explains why we do not report operating-point false-alarm rates from that
design at all, and the same reasoning applies to any single number drawn
from it. What we can say is narrower: scene content that is homogeneous
within a category will produce a content-derived score that looks
consistent across sessions in a way genuine, session-heterogeneous
detector error does not, so a null-detector control can in principle look
\emph{more} stable than the real pipeline even though it carries no
information about perception at all. That is a property of what the
control measures, not a claim about which arm alarmed more often on our
own data --- and it is still exactly why inspecting a monitor's outputs
for smoothness cannot substitute for running the control.

\subsection{Sessions Are Not Exchangeable, and What It Costs Navigation}
\label{sec:discuss-sessions}

Conformal validity is conditional on exchangeability, and driving data
violates it in a structured way: it arrives in sessions that differ
persistently in route, traffic, lighting and weather. Our measurement puts
a number on the cost --- a $3$--$5\times$ per-drive spread in miscoverage
under a pooled quantile, on a nominal-drive sample thin enough (four
held-out drives) that we do not lean on the exact multiple, with
between-session variation comparable in size to the weather effect being
sought.

The consequence is not a modest loss of efficiency. It is that the
monitored quantity is dominated by which session the vehicle is on, so the
monitor detects session identity. For a navigation stack this is the worst
available failure mode. An alarm that tracks route rather than perception
fires on an unremarkable drive and stays silent on a dangerous one, and a
planner wired to react --- by slowing, handing back, or initiating a
minimum-risk manoeuvre --- inherits that error directly and acts on it. A
monitor whose sensitivity has not been demonstrated but which is
nonetheless counted in a safety argument is worse than no monitor, because
the assurance it appears to provide displaces mitigations that would
otherwise be required.

Two practical consequences follow for anyone deploying this pattern. First,
calibration should be conditioned on the session, or at minimum validated
per session rather than in aggregate --- an aggregate coverage check passes
while individual sessions sit far from target. Second, because our own
attempt at the session-conditional fix helped on one statistic and hurt on
another, and reversed direction across gaps within the \emph{same}
checkpoint (Section~\ref{sec:mondrian}), the check has to be repeated
against the model and calibration budget actually being shipped, not
inherited from a paper --- ours included.

\subsection{Underpowered Is Not Absent}
\label{sec:discuss-power}

Our headline measurement is a null we decline to interpret as an absence,
and the distinction carries the section. An interval of $[-0.073, +0.210]$
around a point estimate of $+0.069$ excludes neither a real effect of the
size we previously claimed nor no effect at all. With nine evaluation
sessions the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power is roughly $0.20$,
almost $3\times$ the point estimate, so failing to detect it is what this
design does regardless of whether the effect is there.

The dual-interval reporting in Table~\ref{tab:underpowered} is what makes
that diagnosable rather than fully assertable. Because the nominal-only and
two-level intervals are computed from the same session draws and differ
only in whether the degraded arm is resampled, a reader can see that even
the narrower, nominal-only interval fails to exclude zero on the corrected
data, and that resampling the degraded arm widens it a further
$1.3$--$1.4\times$ on top of that. We suggest reporting both intervals
whenever an effect is estimated from clustered data on both sides; a single
interval says whether a result is significant, the pair helps a reader see
part of why not.

It is worth being explicit that the correction which mattered most for the
point estimate was not the one we expected. Removing the train/evaluation
overlap --- the most serious defect, and the one that voids the earlier
numbers as a matter of principle --- moved the point estimate only modestly
($+0.074 \rightarrow +0.069$ once every correction we can check is applied).
Significance was lost primarily because the drive-disjoint split leaves
only nine sessions to resample, not because the true effect shrank. Both
the leakage and a separate bootstrap bookkeeping bug (Section
\ref{sec:underpowered}) had to be fixed on principle; neither turned out to
be the dominant reason the number stopped being significant.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Scope of the null-detector diagnostic.} It is a null for scores
built from regression residuals. A score reading a different output head
requires its own, and constructing one is not always straightforward: for a
score built from classification-head activation, zeroing that head makes
every score identical and the statistic degenerate, which is a floor rather
than an informative control. We report this because it bounds the
recommendation, and a general recipe --- sever the dependence between the
score and the model output while leaving the calibration pipeline
intact --- is easier to state than to instantiate for every score family.

\paragraph{The diagnostic does not catch every failure.} It catches a
monitor that is not reading the model. It does not catch a monitor that
reads the model but measures the wrong thing --- including the
session-identity failure documented in Section~\ref{sec:exchangeability},
which passes the null-detector check.

\paragraph{Weather is confounded with collection date.} CADC's degraded
drives come from a different date than its nominal ones, so sensor
condition, route and traffic are confounded with weather. The
null-detector control bounds the contribution of label content
specifically; it does not bound the rest. The dataset publishes a
quantitative per-drive snowfall-severity count that varies \emph{within}
date and would break this aliasing; we identified it too late to use, and
regard it as the most promising next step.

\paragraph{Scale and single-run training.} Nine evaluation sessions, one
detector architecture, one dataset carrying the positive findings, and one
training run --- no seed replication, so nothing here has error bars in
that dimension. Training used chunked resumption, so optimizer moment
estimates re-warm at each resume, a small deviation from an uninterrupted
run.

\paragraph{Statistical scope.} The Snowy Scenes intervals in
Section~\ref{sec:snowy-control} use frame-level resampling and are narrower
than our session-level convention would give. The Mondrian worst-case
statistic is a maximum over nine sessions and carries no interval. We
report both rather than presenting the numbers as comparable to the
clustered estimates elsewhere.

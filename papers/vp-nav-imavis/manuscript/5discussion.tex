\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why a Fictitious Monitor Looks Healthy}
\label{sec:discuss-null}

The most uncomfortable result in this paper is that our own reported
detection delays were reproduced exactly, to the frame, by a detector that
outputs nothing. It is worth being precise about why, because the mechanism
generalizes past our particular bug.

The nonconformity score is a residual, $\lVert B_{\mathrm{pred}} -
B_{\mathrm{target}} \rVert_1$, evaluated where a ground-truth object sits.
When the prediction term vanishes the score becomes $\lVert
B_{\mathrm{target}} \rVert_1$: a function of the labels alone. Nothing
downstream notices. Split conformal calibration still returns a
well-defined quantile, because it is a quantile of \emph{some} exchangeable
score distribution. The betting process still accumulates wealth, because
$m(t)$ still moves. The alarm still fires at a plausible time, because
scene content genuinely differs between a dataset's nominal and adverse
splits --- on CADC the mean ground-truth object count per frame roughly
doubles. Every component behaves exactly as specified. The monitor is
measuring the dataset's annotation statistics and reporting them as
perception degradation.

There is an inversion here worth naming. On CADC the null monitor was
\emph{better behaved} than the real one: false-alarm rate $0.00$ against
$1.00$. The reason is that scene content is relatively homogeneous across
nominal drives, so a content-derived score transfers cleanly between
sessions, whereas genuine detector error is heterogeneous across sessions
and does not. The artifact was better behaved than the truth. This inverts
the usual heuristic that cleaner numbers indicate a better method, and it
is precisely why the failure is hard to notice from the outputs alone.

The practical recommendation is cheap. One extra evaluation pass with the
prediction head zeroed, reported as a row in the results table, separates
``our monitor detects degradation'' from ``our monitor detects the
dataset''. We would not have found our own error without it.

\subsection{Sessions Are Not Exchangeable, and What That Costs}
\label{sec:discuss-sessions}

Conformal prediction's guarantee is conditional on exchangeability, and
driving data violates it in a specific, structured way: it arrives in
sessions, and sessions differ in weather, lighting, traffic density and
route in ways that persist across every frame within them. Our measurement
puts a number on the cost --- a $7.5\times$ spread in per-drive miscoverage
under a pooled quantile, with between-session standard deviation ($0.061$)
as large as the weather effect being sought ($0.057$).

The consequence is not a small loss of efficiency. It is that the monitored
quantity is dominated by which session the vehicle is on, so the monitor
detects session identity. That is visible directly in the operating curve:
the marginal baseline's detection delays are negative and become more
negative as the window lengthens, because a longer window simply supplies
more nominal frames from an unlucky session to accumulate against.

Mondrian calibration is the natural repair and it works on one checkpoint.
We report the reversal on the other rather than omitting it, and we
deliberately do not explain it, because we cannot. What we can say is that
the reversal makes an unconditional recommendation unsafe: whether
session-conditional calibration improves worst-case coverage appears to
depend on the model being calibrated, and a practitioner should measure it
on their own model rather than assume it. We would rather publish a
qualified result than a clean one we cannot support at a second
measurement.

\subsection{Monitorability Is Non-Monotone in Detector Quality}
\label{sec:discuss-quality}

The central finding is that the monitorable weather effect is largest at
intermediate detector quality and declines as the detector improves,
becoming undetectable at our best checkpoint.

The mechanism we propose is straightforward, though we test it only
indirectly. A conformal monitor of this design detects a \emph{differential}
--- the gap between nominal-condition error and degraded-condition error.
A collapsed detector has no error differential because it has no error
signal at all, only content. A converged detector has little error
differential because it handles snow nearly as well as clear conditions:
that is what robustness means. The differential is largest for a detector
good enough to have real, structured error but not good enough to be
robust to the condition being monitored.

If this is right, it is an awkward property for the field. The
better-engineered the perception stack, the less there is for a
degradation monitor of this design to find, and the monitor's failure is
silent --- it continues producing calibrated quantiles and plausible alarm
times. A team that improves its detector and keeps its monitor may
reasonably believe both are working.

Three qualifications constrain how far this should be taken. First, power:
two of six points significant, $18$ sessions, wide intervals; the claim
rests on the trend and on the loss of detectability, not on a run of
significant results. Second, the two scores disagree at epoch 9, and we do
not know which is right there. Third, we vary detector quality along one
axis, on one architecture and one dataset; whether the same shape appears
for other detectors or other conditions is untested. The finding is
suggestive and mechanistically plausible, not established.

It also admits a more optimistic reading we cannot presently distinguish
from ours: perhaps a converged detector's residual degradation is real but
concentrated in rarer, harder cases that a frame-averaged miscoverage rate
dilutes away. That would make the problem one of \emph{score design} rather
than of monitorability as such. Our phantom-score result is weak evidence
against the simplest version of that story --- a score built entirely from a
different failure mode also finds nothing at convergence --- but it is not
decisive, and a score targeting rare severe errors specifically is the
experiment we would run next.

\subsection{What the Score Families Tell Us}
\label{sec:discuss-scores}

Adding a phantom-detection component was the only intervention in this
project that changed \emph{what is measured} rather than how we bet on it,
and it is the only one that enlarged the effect: $+54\%$ at $w=1$ relative
to localization alone, with mixing at $w=0.5$ giving the best
effect-to-noise. That the two families are complementary rather than
redundant is a genuine methodological result: spurious activation in empty
space carries weather information that a matched-cell residual cannot see,
which is what one would expect given that snow's dominant LiDAR effect is
spurious near-range returns.

It did not clear the detection bar. Effect-to-noise $0.862$ against the
$0.849$ obtainable without it is within noise of no improvement on that
metric, even though the raw effect is substantially larger --- the
between-session variance grows alongside the effect. We report both numbers
rather than the flattering one.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discuss-limits}

Beyond the power constraints already stated: our evaluation is a single
detector architecture on two datasets, with $18$ nominal sessions on the
dataset carrying the positive result. Training used chunked resumption, so
optimizer moment estimates re-warm at each resume --- a small deviation from
an uninterrupted run, stated because it is a real difference. The
within-session split used for Mondrian places calibration and monitored
frames in the same drive; we control for adjacency with a temporal-gap
sweep and the effect is stable across it, but the split still differs from
the whole-drive protocol used elsewhere in this work, and we flag that
rather than leaving a reader to find the inconsistency. Finally, the
degraded regime on CADC is a different collection date from the nominal
one, so weather is confounded with date; the null-detector control bounds
how much of the effect that could explain, but does not eliminate the
confound.

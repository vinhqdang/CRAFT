\begin{abstract}
Autonomous navigation stacks are built to trust their perception layer, and
increasingly to monitor it: conformal prediction calibrates a nonconformity
score on nominal data, an anytime-valid sequential test tracks its
miscoverage on the live stream, and an alarm tells the planner when
perception has stopped being trustworthy. We set out to reduce the
detection delay of such a monitor for camera/LiDAR 3D detection under real
adverse weather. We did not achieve that. What we report instead are two
validated findings about how monitors of this kind fail silently, and an
honest account of a weather effect we cannot resolve.

The first finding is that a conformal monitor can produce entirely
plausible calibrated quantiles and alarm times while measuring nothing
about the detector at all. If the detector's regression head degenerates,
the nonconformity score $\lVert B_{\mathrm{pred}} - B_{\mathrm{target}}
\rVert_1$ collapses to $\lVert B_{\mathrm{target}} \rVert_1$ --- a function
of the ground-truth labels alone --- and the monitor reports the dataset's
annotation statistics as perception degradation. We introduce a
\emph{null-detector diagnostic} that exposes this: rerun the pipeline with
the prediction head's output replaced by zeros. It needs no additional
data, training or annotation. Applied to our own earlier results it
reproduced them \emph{bit-identically} --- nominal-to-degraded jump
$0.13904668284371138$ from both the real and the zeroed detector, agreeing
to 17 significant figures. A monitor with no detector performed identically
to one with a detector, and on one dataset the null monitor was
\emph{better behaved} than the real one. We recommend this control as
standard practice for conformal monitoring of perception, and we are
explicit about its scope: it is a null for scores built on regression
residuals, and a score reading a different head needs its own null.

The second finding is that split conformal's exchangeability assumption
fails across driving sessions. On the Canadian Adverse Driving Conditions
dataset, per-drive miscoverage under one pooled quantile ranges from
$0.083$ to $0.623$ against a target of $0.20$ --- a $7.5\times$ spread
across drives that are all nominal, all clear-road, several sharing a
collection date --- with between-session variation as large as the weather
effect being sought. The guarantee remains formally correct and becomes
operationally useless: the monitor detects which session the vehicle is on.
For navigation this is the worst available failure mode, because the alarm
fires on route identity rather than on perception loss.

On the weather effect itself we report an \emph{underpowered null}, and
distinguish that carefully from an absence. On drive-disjoint held-out
data with two-level clustered resampling, the effect is $+0.064$ with a
95\% interval of $[-0.061, +0.193]$: consistent with a real effect of the
size we previously claimed, and equally consistent with none. Nine
evaluation sessions cannot resolve it; the minimum detectable effect is
roughly twice the estimate. We also show \emph{why} an earlier version of
this measurement appeared significant, by reporting the interval with and
without the degraded arm resampled --- a decomposition that lets a reader
distinguish "underpowered" from "absent" rather than guess, and which we
suggest reporting alongside any clustered effect estimate of this kind.
\end{abstract}
